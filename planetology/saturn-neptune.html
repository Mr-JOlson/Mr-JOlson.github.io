Question 1

Formation pathway

Both planets grew inside the young Sun’s gas disk, which lasted only a few million years. The leading pathway is core accretion sped up by pebbles: small bits of rock and ice spiral inward and pile onto a core. Saturn’s core reached a tipping point, pebble isolation, and then pulled in a huge hydrogen-helium envelope before the gas disappeared. Neptune grew later and farther out, missed that tipping point, and was left as an ice giant with only a thin blanket of gas.

The distances diverge

Saturn is on the way to pebble isolation while gas is still abundant. The mass needed to shut off pebbles is larger at Neptune’s distance, and the disk will not wait.

Saturn’s envelope collapses

Once pebbles stop, Saturn’s envelope cools and collapses. Gas becomes most of the planet’s mass. Neptune has not reached that tipping point.

Question 2

Collisions and captures

After the gas disk cleared, the giant planets were packed tighter than they are now. An orbital instability moved Uranus and Neptune outward and scattered icy leftovers. Neptune’s headline event is the capture of Triton, whose backward orbit means it was grabbed, not grown in place. Saturn’s headline late event is a possible moon, Chrysalis, pulled apart into the rings about 100 million years ago, plus the capture of the dark outer moon Phoebe.

Giant impacts finish Neptune

Uranus and Neptune likely took their final shape in collisions between large icy embryos beyond Saturn. Those hits help explain two facts: the ice giants have similar masses, and both spin axes are tilted. Neptune’s tilt is about 28 degrees. Uranus was knocked much farther, nearly onto its side. Pebbles alone have trouble finishing two ice giants of almost the same mass on wide orbits. The tilts are the scars.

Stirred core

The notes name a stirred core beside those impacts. They do not give a separate measurement of the mixing. The evidence they point to is the pair of tilts.

Still open. Triton’s capture is on firm ground because a backward orbit is hard to get any other way. Chrysalis explains the young rings and Saturn’s tilt together, but it is still a hypothesis. How many giant impacts Neptune itself took, and whether a fifth ice giant was ejected, are less settled than the fact that the outer planets moved.

Question 3

Volatiles and air

Volatiles here means materials that freeze, melt, or stay gas easily: hydrogen, helium, water, ammonia, and methane. Both planets formed past the snow line, so those ices were part of the building material. Saturn then wrapped them in an enormous hydrogen-helium atmosphere, and helium has been raining inward for billions of years, which helps the planet glow warmer than sunlight alone. Neptune kept most of its mass as hot ice fluids and rock under a thin hydrogen skin tinted blue by methane, and it still leaks enough interior heat to drive extreme winds.

The deep interior is called diffuse. The enrichment in heavy elements, compared with the Sun, is a fossil of the icy solids that arrived first.

Neptune

Outside at the top. Tap a layer.

The visible atmosphere is mostly hydrogen and helium with a few percent methane. Methane absorbs red light, so Neptune looks blue. Hazes change the exact shade, which is why Neptune and Uranus are not the same blue. Gases such as carbon monoxide sit out of balance with this cold air, a sign that winds mix gas up from much hotter depths.

The thin gas envelope is the firm contrast with Saturn. About 1 to 4 Earth masses of hydrogen and helium, and the sketch uses 2, out of 17.

Most of the mass is hot ice fluids under that skin. Interior heat still leaks out and drives extreme winds.

Rock is in there, poorly separated from the ice. The ice-to-rock ratio is poorly known.

Still open. Neptune’s ice-to-rock ratio is poorly known. We have orbited Saturn for years with Cassini, from 2004 to 2017. Neptune has had one flyby, Voyager 2 in 1989. The thin gas envelope is the firm contrast. The exact recipe under it is not.

Question 4

A constrained future

Neither planet is on a path to be swallowed or ejected. Saturn’s rings drain away on a much shorter clock, a few hundred million years. Triton spirals inward and may be pulled apart into a ring a few billion years from now. When the Sun becomes a red giant in about 5 billion years, it swells to roughly Earth’s orbit, far inside both planets. After the Sun becomes a white dwarf and loses about half its mass, the orbits widen to a bit less than double, and the planets cool again around a faint remnant star.

Firm means the direction is hard to avoid. Bounded means we know the kind of outcome and a rough window, not a calendar date.

Firm · both

The baseline

Saturn is at 9.5 AU. Neptune is at 30 AU. Studies of the orbits find that both stay bound to the Sun through the rest of its life as a normal star. Their paths wobble. They are not expected to swap places or escape.

Firm · Saturn

Ring rain

The rings are already losing ice into Saturn. Cassini measured that drain, called ring rain. The rings go on a clock of a few hundred million years, much shorter than the planets.

Firm direction · Neptune

Triton spirals in

Triton orbits backward, so tides pull it inward rather than outward. That spiral has been underway for billions of years. It may be pulled apart into a ring a few billion years from now.

Firm · both

Red giant

In about 5 billion years the Sun swells to roughly Earth’s orbit. That edge is far inside Saturn at 9.5 AU and Neptune at 30 AU. Neither planet is swallowed.

Bounded

White dwarf

The Sun loses about half its mass. The orbits widen to a bit less than double. Both planets cool again around a faint remnant star.

Still open. Triton’s end date and the exact widened orbits depend on numbers we have not measured well: how strongly Neptune’s interior tides grip the moon, and precisely how the Sun loses mass. What is constrained is the direction of each change, and that both planets survive it.
